Where is the leak, and how many sensors do we need to know?

Click any junction to put a leak there, choose how many pressure sensors the utility installs, and watch a probabilistic locator narrow the search. Every pressure value comes from EPANET hydraulic simulations of the Net3 benchmark network; the locator runs live in your browser.

What existed

  • Hydraulic simulators: EPANET, its Python wrapper WNTR, and EPyT-Flow, which generates leak scenarios on 16 networks and ships 7 detection benchmarks.
  • Leak detection is largely solved in simulation: in the BattLeDIM 2020 competition (18 teams, L-Town network) detection accuracy above 95 % was common.
  • Localization models: published graph-neural-network locators with open code, and sensor-placement methods based on information theory and value of information.
  • What these leave open for a utility: a direct answer to “with N sensors, how much pipe do we still have to search, and how sure is that?”

What this work adds

  • A decision view: sensor count and placement turned into metres of pipe to search, with the search region drawn on the map and its coverage measured.
  • A diagnosed noise model: pump switching caused 10–30 m false residuals in 58 of 205 leak-free runs; giving the model the SCADA pump schedule removes them. Removing the network-wide common mode cuts demand noise from 0.082 m to 0.018 m (4.5×).
  • A probabilistic locator with honest checks: Bayesian matching with an empirical noise covariance; noise samples for covariance and for test draws are kept separate; the 80 % search region’s coverage is measured below, not assumed.
  • Greedy sensor placement optimized for the same objective the utility cares about: expected pipe distance to the leak.
  • Verified shortcut: leak response plus demand noise superpose within 0.038 m (median, about 8 % of the leak signal), which is what lets this page answer what-if questions in milliseconds.

Not done yet: a graph-neural-network locator benchmarked against this baseline, robustness to wrong pipe roughness, and the 782-junction L-Town network.

How it works

  • Leak = an EPANET emitter, Q = C·√p, sized at 30 m pressure. Hydraulics are pressure-driven and use the nominal pump schedule, as a utility’s SCADA would supply.
  • Observation = mean 24 h pressure minus the utility’s leak-free model, at the sensors.
  • Locator: for every junction and 11 leak sizes, compare the common-mode-free residual with the simulated signature under the empirical noise covariance; sum over sizes; normalize.

What limits it

  • Simulation only. Real networks add model error (roughness, demands, valve states) that is not modelled yet.
  • Net3 has 92 junctions and serves about L/s, so a 2 L/s leak is under 0.3 % of flow, and small leaks stay near the noise floor.
  • The demand-noise slider scales stored noise samples linearly; the stored samples are 5 %.

Numbers behind this page

  • EPANET runs: 92 leak junctions × 6 sizes, 400 noise-only runs, 120 superposition checks.
  • Superposition error: median m, 95th percentile m.
